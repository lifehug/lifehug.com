The Front Row Index: Inflation, Scarcity, and the Second Life of Deftones

Dave Taylor · 24 November 2026

The first sign came through a closed door. Walking past the bedroom of my daughter Charlee, newly sixteen, I heard Deftones, loud enough to recognize from the hallway. I was excited before I was anything else. She had found a band, and I assumed she was about to step into the world I grew up in: frequent local shows, small rooms, and a band that sounded like no one else.

I was nearly 16 myself, a teenager in Mesa, Arizona, when Around the Fur came out on October 28, 1997, and that record is where I came in. For the next few years I went to as many shows as a pizza-shop paycheck allowed. So I went to buy us tickets for the 2027 tour, and the Hollywood Bowl stopped me cold: box and front sections listed on the resale market at roughly $500 to more than $1,000 a seat, and the pool circle near the stage at nearly $2,000.

My first reaction was the reflex of every parent who remembers a cheaper world: this is just inflation, and I am just old. The numbers turned out to be more interesting than that. Charlee is the same age I was when I found this band, but she has not found the band I found, and she is not walking into the world I walked into. She has discovered a new band, in a new world.

The inflation baseline

The fairest starting point is a real ticket from the same band in the same era, and the record offers two. On December 5, 1997, five weeks after Around the Fur was released, Deftones played Clutch Cargo's, a club in Pontiac, Michigan, with Limp Bizkit; a ticket was $13. Three years later, on November 28, 2000, during the White Pony cycle, they headlined the Knoxville Civic Coliseum with Incubus and Taproot for $27.50. Neither is an Arizona show, and I was at neither, but together they bracket my own years with the band: a club act in 1997, an arena act by 2000.

The arena ticket is the fairer comparison, since Deftones now play arenas and amphitheaters. Fifty-four dollars is the number inflation is entitled to. Anything above it has to be explained by something else.

The table tells a story that a single average would hide. The cheapest seat has roughly kept pace with prices in general; a fan willing to sit far back pays about one and a half times an inflation-adjusted 2000 ticket. Even the box office's own face values stay within a few multiples. The distortion lives in the upper part of the house and on the secondary market, where the typical seat runs three to five times the baseline and the seats nearest the stage run ten to nearly forty times. Inflation accounts for the floor. It does not account for the ceiling.

Wages make the point more sharply, because what a ticket costs is ultimately measured in hours of work.

In 1997 a Deftones club ticket cost about one fortieth of a median week, and in 2000 an arena ticket about one twentieth. Today the back of the room costs only slightly more of a week. The middle of the market costs a fifth of a week, and the front costs half a week to a week and a half. The concert did not get uniformly more expensive. It was stratified.

How the business changed

The trend is not unique to one band. Pollstar has tracked the average ticket for North America's top 100 tours since 1996, when it was $25.81. Had it merely followed the CPI, it would be about $55 today. It was $134.23 in 2025.

The first force is concentration. When one company's events draw 159 million people a year, pricing stops being a guess and becomes an optimization problem. Dynamic pricing, platinum tiers, and VIP bundles are tools for discovering exactly what the most motivated buyer will pay, and then charging it. In 2000 the price of a seat was printed on the stub and left alone; the gap between what a ticket cost and what a fan would have paid was simply kept by the fan. The modern system is designed to close that gap, and the front of the house is where it closes most completely.

The second force is who the buyers are. Gen Z now outspends every other generation on live music, and three quarters of them say they would pay premium resale prices to get in. For a generation that rents its music by the month and owns almost none of it, the live show has become the scarce, ownable experience: the thing that cannot be streamed.

The third force is structural. The market has split by room size.

The club sells proximity to music. The arena sells proximity to an event, with the social proof and the sense of having been there that come with it. The small room still prices like the world I grew up in. The catch, for anyone hoping to see Deftones the old way, is that they no longer play clubs on headline runs. Their audience has outgrown the tier where the old economics survive.

Even within the 2027 tour, the spread is wide. The 24-show run is built mostly around amphitheaters; the exceptions are the Hollywood Bowl, Forest Hills Stadium, and San Antonio's Frost Bank Center, an indoor arena.

It's not just inflation: the curious case of Deftones

All of this would describe any major act. What makes Deftones unusual is that the demand behind these prices is substantially new.

The engine is not a reunion, a new member, a biopic, or a marketing campaign. Around 2022, "Cherry Waves," a deep cut from 2006's Saturday Night Wrist, became a TikTok phenomenon, followed by "Sextape" and a run of older songs. Louder reported that the #deftones hashtag drew eight billion views from January 2022 onward. CAA agent Jared Martin told Pollstar that more than half of a typical recent audience were newer fans in their late teens and early twenties, an estimate rather than a census, but consistent with the streaming curve. Chino Moreno has described the growth as unsolicited, and suggests that is part of why it reads as genuine.

The result is a rare arc: a band reaching its commercial peak three decades after its debut, not by reviving its old audience but by recruiting a new one that was not born when White Pony came out.

One discrepancy deserves attention. The 2025 box office implies an average of about $93 per seat, while today's resale medians run $165 to $271. The first is what tickets were issued for; the second is what the market will bear once demand outruns supply. The gap between them is, in a sense, the subject of this essay.

There is also a domestic detail I find more persuasive than any chart. Drummer Abe Cunningham told Kerrang! that the band now meets parents and teenagers together, and hears both answers to who introduced whom. The resurgence is not only generational replacement. In some households it is generational overlap.

How rare is a second rise?

A crude but honest test is to compare Spotify audiences before and after. Spotify itself grew enormously in these years, so every band's number rose; the question is by how much. Deftones grew roughly 8.5 times. Linkin Park, System of a Down, Korn, Muse, Slipknot, and Incubus grew between 2.2 and 3.1 times. The only comparable multiple belongs to Kate Bush, whose single song reached a new audience through Stranger Things.

Kate Bush and Fleetwood Mac show that a single song can leap generations, but those revivals lived on charts and dashboards; they did not fill arenas with sixteen-year-olds. Linkin Park did fill stadiums, but with a clear narrative event behind it. Deftones combine the organic, catalog-driven discovery of the first two with the box-office conversion of the third, and without a catalyst. That is not proof that nothing like it has happened before, but it is a much narrower category than "old band gets popular again."

Why this band? Stereogum noted that the songs resonating with young listeners are the band's most shoegaze-adjacent ones, arriving just as a shoegaze revival took hold on the same platform. PopMatters observed that algorithmic feeds flatten time, placing a 2006 deep cut beside this week's releases. And Moreno has said the band worked deliberately, even during the nu metal boom, to avoid sounding tied to a moment. Music built to resist dating turns out to suit an environment that ignores dates entirely.

The Muse problem

The clearest counterfactual is Muse. If you had asked me which band from that era would have the more durable mainstream presence, I would have said Muse without hesitation: stadium-scale ambition, broader name recognition, a more obviously global footprint.

Muse has not declined; it has persisted. It still has the larger streaming audience, a deeper international base, and a catalog that never went out of circulation. What it lacks is a second curve. Its audience grew up with it, while Deftones' audience was replaced and multiplied, closing a gap of nearly four to one in 2020 to almost even today. In network terms, Muse kept its connections, and Deftones acquired a new graph.

A new world

Charlee is the age I was in 1997, and the name on her speaker is the same one that was on mine. It is not the same band. To her, Deftones are simply current, as present as anything in her feed, and the price of seeing them is the price of something she values. I grew up in an economy where the show was the cheap part and the record was the investment. She is growing up in the inverse, where the music is nearly free and the room is the luxury.

On Wednesday, May 19, 2027, I am taking her to see them at the Hollywood Bowl. Somewhere in that bowl there will be a man my age doing this same arithmetic, standing next to a teenager who has never once thought about it. That, more than any multiple in these tables, is what has changed.
